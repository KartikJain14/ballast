The Problem

Two places for a dollar to sit. Neither is free.

Deposits and withdrawals of roughly $10M each move through the bank every hour. Every dollar sitting in the everyday checking account is instantly safe — and earns nothing. Every dollar moved into the overnight vault earns interest — but can't be pulled back without a penalty if a customer needs it unexpectedly.

What the Data Actually Showed

Not every night is the same night.

We measured the worst-case overnight cash drain — hour by hour — for all 364 nights in the year. A single flat "safety cushion" turned out to be the wrong idea entirely:

Friday and Saturday nights see 4–5× the cash drain of any other night — and it has nothing to do with payday. It's simply when people spend. A bank sizing its cushion the same way every night either wastes money sitting idle five nights a week, or gets caught short on the two that matter. Ballast sizes the cushion separately for weeknights and weekend nights — the single biggest design decision in the system.

The Full-Year Result

Four ways to run the same bank.

We ran the entire year four times: never sweeping a dollar, a well-tuned fixed daily cushion, Ballast's adaptive sizing, and a theoretical perfect-foresight upper bound no real system could ever reach. Lower is better — this is total cost of liquidity: interest left on the table plus every fee, haircut, and penalty paid.

Once a bank sweeps at all, most of the value is already captured — that's the big jump from "never sweep" to "sweep with a sensible fixed cushion." Ballast's edge over a well-tuned static cushion is real but modest in calm conditions (it's a genuinely small economic band — we checked, not assumed). Where Ballast actually earns its keep is the Storm Test above: the same day-of-week awareness that saves a little money on a quiet Tuesday is what keeps the right amount in reserve on the Friday night that isn't quiet at all.
